# The Moral Case For and Against Private Equity

**Purpose:** A structured dialectic investigation of the moral case against and for leveraged-buyout private equity, with particular attention to the claim that PE firms "raid" the pension funds of acquired companies. Part I presents the case against; Part II the case for; Part III synthesizes point-by-point without rendering moral verdicts, adding contextual support on the second-order effects of accepting or rejecting each argument. The Appendix is a neutral factual brief on the pension question.

**Method:** Produced 2026-08-04 by three context-isolated research agents — one instructed to build the strongest case against, one the strongest case for (neither saw the other's work), and one neutral fact-finder on the pension record — followed by a synthesis pass that pairs the arguments and flags where the factual record supports, undercuts, or cannot resolve each side's claims. Parts I and II are advocacy by design: each argument is deliberately stated in its strongest form, so neither part should be read alone. Citations were checked against web sources by the research agents but should be independently verified before being relied on.

---

# Part I. The Case Against Private Equity

**A1. Pension stripping: forty years of converting workers' deferred wages into investor gains**

*Moral basis:* Fiduciary duty, desert, exploitation, breach of implicit contract. A pension is deferred compensation — wages workers already earned and left in trust. Taking it is not sharp dealing; it is taking someone else's property.

*The argument:* The buyout industry's relationship to worker pensions is not a series of accidents but a repeated, structural pattern across two eras. **Era one (1980s):** overfunded defined-benefit plans made companies takeover targets precisely *because* the surplus could be harvested. Acquirers terminated healthy plans, bought the cheapest annuities they could find to cover accrued benefits, and pocketed the "surplus" — money that existed only because workers had accepted lower cash wages in exchange for retirement security. GAO studied this directly ("Pension Plans: Terminations, Asset Reversions, and Replacements Following Leveraged Buyouts," HRD-91-21); roughly $20 billion was reverted from pension plans in the period, with more than $16 billion since 1981 going to fund mergers, LBOs, and other non-retirement uses. The practice was so abusive that Congress had to kill it with a 50% excise tax in 1990 — a legislative verdict that this was looting, not management. The named cases are damning: after David Murdock's 1982 leveraged takeover of **Cannon Mills**, he terminated the ~$100 million pension plan, took roughly $36–37 million for himself, and parked workers' annuities with junk-bond-stuffed **Executive Life** — which collapsed in 1991 (the largest insurance failure in U.S. history), cutting retired millworkers' checks. Charles Hurwitz's MAXXAM did the same after the 1986 **Pacific Lumber** takeover: terminated the plan, captured ~$55–62 million in "surplus," and — over negative evaluations — insisted on Executive Life's lowball annuity bid ($2.7 million below the next-lowest) because the cheaper the annuity, the bigger the reversion (*Kayes v. Pacific Lumber*, 51 F.3d 1449, 9th Cir. 1995). Workers' retirement security was gambled on junk bonds to enlarge the raider's take.

**Era two (modern):** when the surplus-reversion door closed, the mechanism inverted — instead of taking overfunded plans' assets, sponsors dump *underfunded* plans on the public. **Sun Capital** bought Friendly's Ice Cream, drove it into 2011 bankruptcy, and attempted to sell the chain to *another Sun Capital affiliate* while shedding the pensions of ~6,000 workers and retirees onto the PBGC — a maneuver the PBGC itself characterized as an abuse and fought. Sun's **Marsh Supermarkets** ended the same way: Sun extracted value (including real-estate sales) during ownership, and when Marsh liquidated in 2017 the pension was ~$76 million underfunded — the Washington Post's headline captured it: "As a grocery chain is dismantled, investors recover their money. Worker pensions are short millions" (Dec. 2018). And when multiemployer plans tried to make PE funds stand behind their portfolio companies' pension promises, the industry litigated its way out: in *Sun Capital Partners III v. New England Teamsters* (1st Cir. 2019), Sun's funds — which owned 100% of bankrupt Scott Brass between them and actively managed it — escaped $4.5 million in withdrawal liability through deliberate structuring (splitting ownership 70/30 across two funds to dodge the 80% "common control" threshold). The moral through-line across both eras: PE claims the upside of controlling companies while engineering legal distance from the single most solemn obligation those companies carry. The counterparties are not sophisticated investors; they are textile workers, loggers, waitresses, and grocery clerks whose only sin was trusting that earned benefits would be paid.

*Evidence:* GAO HRD-91-21 (1991) and HRD-90-126 (1990); ~$20B in 1980s reversions (PBGC figures); OBRA 1990's 50% reversion excise tax; Cannon Mills/Murdock (Facing South, "Through the Mill," 1991; Newsweek, "Is Your Pension Fund Safe?"); *Kayes v. Pacific Lumber* (9th Cir. 1995); PBGC objections in Friendly's bankruptcy (2011); Marsh Supermarkets (Washington Post, Dec. 28, 2018; Indianapolis Business Journal); *Sun Capital v. New England Teamsters*, 943 F.3d 49 (1st Cir. 2019).

**A2. The leverage model is engineered asymmetry: others bear the risk, the sponsor takes the gains**

*Moral basis:* Fairness, moral hazard, exploitation of counterparties who never consented to the risk.

*The argument:* The LBO's defining move is that the *acquired company* — not the acquirer — borrows the purchase price. The PE firm puts in a thin equity slice, the target's own assets collateralize the debt, and the target's cash flow services it. From day one the firm can then extract cash regardless of company performance: **dividend recapitalizations** (borrow more against the company to pay the owners a dividend), **sale-leasebacks** (sell the company's real estate, distribute the proceeds, and saddle the company with permanent rent on buildings it used to own), and **monitoring/transaction fees** the sponsor charges its own portfolio company. Each device converts the company's resilience into the sponsor's cash. If the hollowed company survives, the sponsor keeps everything; if it collapses, the losses land on workers, trade creditors, landlords, pensioners, and communities. This is not risk-taking — it is risk-*transfer* dressed as risk-taking. The cases are textbook: **Payless ShoeSource** — after Golden Gate Capital and Blum Capital's 2012 LBO, the owners paid themselves ~$350–400 million in dividends while loading the chain with debt; it filed for bankruptcy in 2017 with $838 million of debt, and creditors (recovering 17–21 cents on the dollar) alleged the dividends were siphoning — the owners settled. **Simmons Bedding** — serially flipped by PE owners (Thomas H. Lee took ~$77 million in dividends plus fees), its debt multiplied over successive buyouts until its 2009 bankruptcy wiped out bondholders and cost more than a thousand workers their jobs (Julie Creswell, "Profits for Buyout Firms as Company Debt Soared," New York Times, Oct. 4, 2009). **Steward Health Care** — Cerberus had Steward sell its hospital real estate to Medical Properties Trust in 2016 for $1.25 billion, recouped its investment and paid dividends, and exited with roughly **$800 million in profit** while the hospital system, crushed by $6.6 billion in lease obligations on buildings it once owned, slid into its 2024 bankruptcy. And Phalippou, Rauch & Umber (*Journal of Financial Economics*, 2018) documented **$20 billion** in monitoring and transaction fees charged by PE firms to 592 of their own portfolio companies — fees unrelated to any service rendered, extracted from companies whose boards the fee-recipients controlled, a naked self-dealing conflict.

*Evidence:* Payless creditor filings and settlement (Reuters, June 2017; bankruptcy filings, E.D. Mo.); Simmons (Creswell, NYT 2009, "Flipped" series); Steward/Cerberus/MPT (Private Equity Stakeholder Project, "The Pillaging of Steward Health Care," 2024; Boston Globe; Senate Warren-Brown letters, 2024); Phalippou, Rauch & Umber, "Private Equity Portfolio Company Fees," *JFE* 129(3), 2018.

**A3. In healthcare, the model measurably kills and injures people**

*Moral basis:* Harm — the gravest kind; exploitation of the sick, the old, and the captive.

*The argument:* The strongest empirical indictment of PE is that when its extraction model meets patients, the casualties are counted in bodies, not dollars. Gupta, Howell, Yannelis & Gupta (NBER w28474, 2021; published in *Review of Financial Studies*, 2024) studied nursing homes with patient-level Medicare data and found PE ownership **increased short-term mortality by 10%** — implying roughly **21,000 excess deaths** over 2000–2017 — while *increasing* taxpayer-billed spending by 19%. The mechanism was exactly the extraction playbook: cuts to front-line nursing staff, increased anti-psychotic sedation, declining mobility, and compliance failures — costs cut where the payer (Medicare/Medicaid) can't see and the customer (a frail elder) can't leave. Kannan, Bruch & Song (*JAMA*, Dec. 2023) found hospitals acquired by PE saw a **25.4% increase in hospital-acquired conditions** — 27% more falls, 38% more central-line bloodstream infections — across 662,095 hospitalizations at 51 PE hospitals vs. matched controls. In emergency medicine, Cooper, Scott Morton & Shekita (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2020) showed that when KKR-owned **EmCare** took over hospital ERs, out-of-network billing jumped by over 81 percentage points and charges nearly doubled — the "surprise billing" crisis that Congress had to outlaw was substantially a PE revenue strategy (EmCare/Envision–KKR, TeamHealth–Blackstone), and those firms then bankrolled dark-money ads against the No Surprises Act. The institutional wreckage is named: Carlyle's **HCR ManorCare** (real estate sold for $6.1 billion; health-code violations rose 26% before its 2018 bankruptcy — Washington Post investigation, 2018); **Hahnemann University Hospital**, a 170-year-old Philadelphia safety-net hospital bought in 2018 by investor Joel Freedman, its real estate separated for redevelopment, closed within 18 months; and **Steward**, whose 31 hospitals entered bankruptcy with $9 billion in liabilities after Cerberus's $800 million exit — with documented shortages of basic supplies while dividends flowed. A model that reliably produces excess mortality when applied to care settings is not being unluckily misapplied; it is doing what it is built to do — strip cost and extract cash — in a domain where cost is care.

*Evidence:* Gupta et al., "Owner Incentives and Performance in Healthcare: Private Equity Investment in Nursing Homes," NBER w28474 / *RFS* 37(4), 2024; Kannan, Bruch & Song, *JAMA* 330(24), 2023; Cooper, Scott Morton & Shekita, *JPE* 128(9), 2020; Washington Post ManorCare investigation (2018); PESP Steward report (2024); Warren/Brown Senate letters on Steward (2024).

**A4. PE ownership makes bankruptcy ten times more likely — and bankruptcy is a machine for externalizing losses**

*Moral basis:* Harm, fairness; imposing uncompensated ruin on third parties.

*The argument:* Defenders call bankruptcies unfortunate outliers. The data say otherwise. Ayash & Rastad (*Finance Research Letters*, 2020; SSRN 2019) tracked 484 public-to-private LBOs for ten years post-buyout: **~20% went bankrupt, versus ~2%** of matched public controls — an order of magnitude difference attributable to the capital structure the sponsor chose. In retail the rate reached 41% (1980–95 cohort). And crucially, PE bankruptcy is often not failure *for the sponsor*: through dividends, fees, and asset sales taken out early, the fund can profit on a deal whose company dies — Payless, Marsh, Simmons, Steward, and **Toys "R" Us** all fit this pattern. Toys "R" Us was servicing $450–500 million a year in interest on the ~$6.6 billion in debt from its 2005 KKR/Bain/Vornado buyout — debt that consumed the capital it needed to compete with Amazon — and when it liquidated in 2018, **33,000 workers** lost their jobs with no severance; only after a public campaign did KKR and Bain fund a $20 million hardship fund, a fraction of the ~$75 million owed under the severance policy. Bankruptcy law then completes the moral inversion: secured lenders and sponsors' claims are honored, executives get retention bonuses, and workers, suppliers, landlords, and pension funds absorb the shortfall. A business model in which one party chooses the leverage, harvests cash along the way, and hands a 10x-elevated ruin probability to everyone else is a model whose profits are partly other people's losses.

*Evidence:* Ayash & Rastad, "Leveraged Buyouts and Financial Distress," *Finance Research Letters* (2020); Toys "R" Us (In These Times, "How Private Equity Killed Toys 'R' Us," 2018; Congressional Progressive Caucus letter, July 2018; Appelbaum & Batt, *The American Prospect*, "Private Equity — Looting 'R' Us," 2018).

**A5. The employment and wage evidence: workers fund the returns**

*Moral basis:* Exploitation, breach of implicit contracts, distributive fairness.

*The argument:* The definitive census-data study — Davis, Haltiwanger, Handley, Lipsius, Lerner & Miranda, "The (Heterogeneous) Economic Effects of Private Equity Buyouts" (NBER w26371; thousands of U.S. buyouts, 1980–2013) — finds that in the classic leveraged buyout of a public company, **employment at targets falls 13% over two years** relative to controls, and **average earnings per worker fall 1.7%**, "largely erasing a pre-buyout wage premium." That last phrase is the moral core: the pre-buyout wage premium is the implicit contract — loyalty, tenure, above-market pay for above-market commitment — and the buyout's "efficiency gain" consists substantially of confiscating it. This is Shleifer and Summers' (1988) "breach of trust" thesis made empirical: takeover premia are partly financed not by creating value but by transferring it from stakeholders who cannot renegotiate. Note what this does to the industry's favorite defense: the productivity gains the same study finds (+8%) flow to the fund and its lenders, while the measured wage change for retained workers is *negative*. The 1.3 million retail jobs lost at PE- and hedge-fund-owned retailers over the preceding decade ("Pirate Equity," PESP/United for Respect, 2019 — during a period when retail employment overall *grew*) fell disproportionately on women and workers of color. Workers bear the layoffs, the wage compression, and the bankruptcy risk; they hold no equity, signed no LBO, and were never asked.

*Evidence:* Davis et al., NBER w26371 (rev. 2019/2024); Davis et al., "Private Equity and Employment," NBER w17399 (2011); Shleifer & Summers, "Breach of Trust in Hostile Takeovers," in *Corporate Takeovers* (NBER, 1988); "Pirate Equity" (PESP/United for Respect, July 2019; Washington Post coverage, July 24, 2019).

**A6. The social bargain fails: all this harm buys no outperformance — PE is a billionaire factory, not a value factory**

*Moral basis:* Desert; proportionality — harms might arguably be tolerated for great social gains, but the gains don't exist.

*The argument:* Every harm above is defended with one claim: PE's discipline creates value that justifies the pain. Ludovic Phalippou (Oxford, "An Inconvenient Fact: Private Equity Returns & The Billionaire Factory," *Journal of Investing*, 2020) tested it: across the major datasets, PE funds since 2006 returned **~11% per annum net — matching ordinary public equity indices** — meaning a pension fund would have done as well in a Vanguard index fund, without the illiquidity, the opacity, or the wreckage. Yet for delivering index-level returns, GPs extracted an estimated **$230 billion in performance fees**, and the number of PE multibillionaires went from 3 (2005) to 22 (2020). So the industry's whole apparatus — the leverage that decuples bankruptcy risk, the 13% job cuts, the nursing-home staffing cuts, the pension dumps — generates *no net risk-adjusted surplus for the capital owners*, only a colossal private transfer to fund managers. Under any desert-based ethic, an industry whose distinctive activities produce enormous third-party harm and zero excess value for its own investors, while minting billionaires from fees, is not earning its returns. It is a wealth-redistribution engine pointed upward.

*Evidence:* Phalippou (2020), SSRN 3623820 / *Journal of Investing*; net multiples ~1.55–1.63 across Burgiss, Preqin, Cambridge datasets; Institutional Investor profile, "How Ludovic Phalippou Became the Bête Noire of Private Equity."

**A7. PE systematically fleeces its own investors — who are teachers' and firefighters' pension funds**

*Moral basis:* Fiduciary duty, honesty, consent vitiated by deception.

*The argument:* The industry's claim to legitimacy-by-consent ("sophisticated investors chose this") collapses on the SEC's own findings. In 2014, Andrew Bowden, then director of the SEC's exam program, reported after examining 150+ PE managers that in **over half** of exams the SEC found "violations of law or material weaknesses in controls" around fees and expenses — hidden fees, expenses shifted to LPs, undisclosed acceleration of monitoring fees paid out on exit, terms in limited partnership agreements that no investor could police ("Spreading Sunshine in Private Equity," May 6, 2014). A majority-noncompliance rate is not a few bad apples; it is an industry norm of skimming. Add the $20 billion in portfolio-company fees (A2) that dilute LP returns, and IRRs inflated by subscription credit lines and self-marked interim valuations, and the picture is one of pervasive information exploitation. The victims complete the moral circle: the LPs are overwhelmingly **public pension funds** — CalPERS famously admitted in 2015 it could not say how much carry it had paid its PE managers. The same industry that strips workers' pensions at the portfolio-company level also overcharges workers' pensions at the fund level. When the SEC finally adopted its Private Fund Adviser rules (2023) requiring basic fee disclosure and banning preferential secret terms, the industry sued and got them vacated (*NAFPM v. SEC*, 5th Cir., June 2024) — fighting in court for the right not to tell its own fiduciary clients what it charges them.

*Evidence:* Bowden speech, SEC.gov, May 6, 2014; Phalippou, Rauch & Umber (2018); CalPERS carry-tracking admission (widely reported, 2015); *National Association of Private Fund Managers v. SEC* (5th Cir. 2024).

**A8. Carried interest: the industry's income is mislabeled to dodge taxes, and it buys the politics to keep it that way**

*Moral basis:* Fairness, civic reciprocity, equality before the law.

*The argument:* Carried interest is compensation for managing other people's money — labor income by any economic definition — yet it is taxed at capital-gains rates (~23.8% top all-in) instead of ordinary rates (~37% plus payroll taxes). Victor Fleischer's foundational analysis ("Two and Twenty: Taxing Partnership Profits in Private Equity Funds," *NYU Law Review* 83:1, 2008) called it the most tax-efficient compensation available to any executive class in America; recent estimates put the cost of the mislabeling at tens of billions per decade (Yale Budget Lab: up to ~$87.7 billion/10 years under updated methods). The moral offense is double. First, desert and equality: a nurse in a PE-owned hospital pays a higher marginal rate on her wages than the fund partner pays on the "carry" generated by cutting her staffing. Second, corruption of process: the loophole has survived every president of both parties who promised to close it — surviving 2017 and 2022 reconciliation bills after targeted industry lobbying — which means the industry's excess wealth is partly recycled into purchasing the tax treatment that produces the excess wealth. An industry whose margin depends on a legislated mislabeling of its income, defended by lobbying, cannot claim its rewards are the verdict of a fair market.

*Evidence:* Fleischer, *NYU L. Rev.* (2008); Tax Policy Center briefing book on carried interest; Yale Budget Lab, "Refining Revenue Estimates for Taxing Carried Interest"; CBPP, "An Analysis of the 'Carried Interest' Controversy" (2007).

**A9. Opacity and regulatory arbitrage are the product, not a byproduct**

*Moral basis:* Consent, honesty, rule-of-law reciprocity — profiting from engineered blindness.

*The argument:* Public companies disclose financials, executive pay, and material risks; their acquirers face securities law, exchange rules, and shareholder suits. PE's core arbitrage is taking productive assets *out* of that accountability regime while remaining economically identical: same workers, same customers, same social footprint — minus the visibility. Inside the dark: self-reported interim valuations that smooth losses; IRRs mechanically inflated by subscription lines and early dividend recaps; GP-led "continuation fund" sales where the sponsor sits on both sides of the transaction and sets the price of assets it sells from one of its funds to another. The industry's legal architecture is equally deliberate: the fund, the GP, the management company, and each portfolio company are separate limited-liability boxes, so that — as the *Sun Capital* litigation proved — the entity that makes every decision bears none of the obligations (pensions, torts, wage claims) its decisions create. And whenever transparency threatens, the industry litigates or lobbies it away: the vacated 2023 SEC private-fund rules (A7), the fights against state healthcare-transaction review laws, the dark-money campaign against surprise-billing reform. A sector that reorganizes ever-larger swaths of the economy — hospitals, housing, insurance — specifically into structures that regulators, counterparties, and the public cannot see is not neutrally "private"; it is monetizing the difference between what it knows and what everyone it deals with is permitted to know.

*Evidence:* *Sun Capital* (1st Cir. 2019) structuring-to-avoid-liability; *NAFPM v. SEC* (5th Cir. 2024); Bowden (2014) on unmonitorable LPA terms; Phalippou (2020) on IRR inflation; Cooper/Scott Morton/Shekita and Arnold Ventures reporting on the "Doctor Patient Unity" dark-money campaign against surprise-billing legislation (~$28 million, 2019, funded by Blackstone- and KKR-backed physician staffing firms).

**A10. Extracting rents from the necessities of the vulnerable: housing**

*Moral basis:* Exploitation of captivity; harm to those least able to exit.

*The argument:* After 2008, PE moved into the one asset everyone must consume: shelter. The playbook — maximize fee income, minimize maintenance, automate enforcement — was applied to tenants the way it was applied to portfolio companies. The FTC's 2024 action against **Invitation Homes** (built by Blackstone into the nation's largest single-family landlord) documented the model: advertising rents that excluded mandatory "junk fees," tens of millions in undisclosed charges (2021–23), unreturned security deposits, un-inspected homes, and — during the pandemic — steering tenants *away* from the CDC declaration that would have protected them from eviction and toward the company's own affidavit, which protected nothing. Invitation Homes paid **$48 million** and accepted conduct restraints. Peer-reviewed work backs the pattern: Raymond et al. (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, 2016) found large corporate single-family landlords in Atlanta filed evictions at far higher rates than small landlords — some institutional owners serving eviction notices on roughly a third of their tenants in a year — using eviction filing as routine rent collection. Mobile-home parks, the last unsubsidized affordable housing, drew PE rollups (Carlyle, Apollo-linked, Stockbridge/YES! Communities) that sharply raised lot rents on homeowners who literally cannot move their homes — the purest case of pricing against captivity. Where a market's "inefficiency" is the forbearance landlords historically showed neighbors, optimizing it away is not value creation; it is the conversion of other people's precarity into yield.

*Evidence:* FTC v. Invitation Homes, stipulated order, N.D. Ga., Sept. 2024 ($48M); FTC press release, Sept. 24, 2024; Raymond, Duckworth, Miller, Lucas & Pokharel, "Corporate Landlords, Institutional Investors, and Displacement," Atlanta Fed Community & Economic Development Discussion Paper 2016-04; reporting on PE mobile-home-park rent escalation (Washington Post/NYT/Private Equity Stakeholder Project, "Private Equity Giants Converge on Manufactured Homes," 2019).

# Part II. The Case For Private Equity

**F1. PE returns flow overwhelmingly to teachers, firefighters, and retirees — and PE has been the best-performing asset in their pension portfolios for over a decade**

*Moral basis:* Welfare, consequences for the worst-off, fiduciary duty fulfilled.

*The argument:* The caricature of private equity casts "Wall Street billionaires" as the beneficiaries. The arithmetic says otherwise. The limited partners who supply the capital and take roughly 80% of the profits are, first among them, public employee pension funds — the retirement security of schoolteachers, police officers, firefighters, and sanitation workers — alongside university endowments, charitable foundations, and insurance pools backing ordinary annuities. Public and private pension funds are the single largest category of PE capital, and U.S. public pensions hold roughly 9% of their portfolios in private equity on a dollar-weighted basis, with major funds like CalPERS (serving over 2 million beneficiaries) allocating close to 18%. For these funds — many chronically underfunded and legally obligated to pay defined benefits — PE returns are not a luxury; they are the margin between honoring pension promises and cutting benefits or raising taxes on the public. A practice whose residual gains disproportionately shore up the retirement of modest-income public servants has a powerful welfare claim.

*Evidence:* The American Investment Council's annual public pension studies find private equity has been **the best-returning asset class in public pension portfolios every year since the study began in 2012**, with a median 10-year annualized return of **13.5% net of fees (2024 study)** — more than 100 basis points above public equities. In the academic literature, Harris, Jenkinson, and Kaplan ("Private Equity Performance: What Do We Know?", *Journal of Finance*, 2014; NBER w17874), using the Burgiss dataset of ~1,400 funds, find U.S. buyout funds **outperformed the S&P 500 by 20–27% over a fund's life, more than 3% annually**, net of fees — confirming and strengthening the earlier Kaplan–Schoar (*Journal of Finance*, 2005) benchmark work. These returns compound directly into pension solvency.

**F2. The governance argument: PE fixes a real and morally costly failure — the waste of other people's money by unaccountable managers**

*Moral basis:* Stewardship, agency, desert (rewards tied to performance), honest use of entrusted resources.

*The argument:* The dispersed-ownership public corporation has a structural moral defect that Michael Jensen diagnosed in "Eclipse of the Public Corporation" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1989) and "Agency Costs of Free Cash Flow" (*American Economic Review*, 1986): managers controlling free cash flow they do not own face weak accountability, and the predictable results are empire-building, entrenchment, cross-subsidized failing divisions, and quiet decay — all paid for by shareholders (i.e., pension funds and index-holding savers) and, eventually, by workers when the bloated firm fails. The LBO structure is a governance repair: concentrated ownership with a controlling board that actually monitors; managers required to invest their own wealth so they succeed only if the firm does; and debt that converts discretionary cash into a hard obligation, forcing managers to justify every dollar retained. This is not financial trickery — it is the restoration of the basic norm that people who control resources should answer to the people who own them. Pay-for-performance under PE is desert-based compensation: managers get rich only by making the company genuinely more valuable.

*Evidence:* Jensen (1989, HBR/SSRN 146149). Kaplan ("The Effects of Management Buyouts on Operating Performance and Value," *Journal of Financial Economics*, 1989) documented large post-buyout operating income and cash flow improvements in 1980s MBOs — evidence the gains came from running firms better, not from accounting. Kaplan and Strömberg ("Leveraged Buyouts and Private Equity," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2009) synthesize the governance-engineering evidence: smaller, more active boards; management equity stakes an order of magnitude larger than at comparable public firms.

---

**F3. Operational value creation is empirically real: PE-owned firms become more productive, better run, and — measurably — cleaner and safer**

*Moral basis:* Welfare (aggregate wealth creation), truth over caricature, excellence as a virtue.

*The argument:* If PE returns came from asset-stripping, the operational data would show deterioration. It shows the opposite, in some of the most careful micro-empirical work in modern finance. Productivity rises at target firms relative to controls; health-and-safety outcomes that no financial engineer can fake — restaurant sanitation inspections, OSHA injury rates — improve after buyouts. This matters morally because productivity growth is the only durable source of rising wages and cheaper goods; a business model that systematically raises the efficiency and operational quality of thousands of mid-sized firms is performing a public service through private means.

*Evidence:* Davis, Haltiwanger, Handley, Lipsius, Lerner, and Miranda ("The Economic Effects of Private Equity Buyouts," NBER w26371, 2019/2021), covering thousands of U.S. buyouts 1980–2013 against millions of control firms via Census microdata, find **large average post-buyout productivity gains** (labor productivity up ~8% relative to controls over two years). Bernstein and Sheen ("The Operational Consequences of Private Equity Buyouts," *Review of Financial Studies*, 2016), using Florida health-inspection records for 50,000+ restaurant locations across 103 chain buyouts, find restaurants become **cleaner, safer, and better maintained** after PE acquisition — with menu *price cuts*, not gouging — and effects concentrated in directly controlled stores, pinning causality on the owner. Cohn, Nestoriak, and Wardlaw ("Private Equity Buyouts and Workplace Safety," *Review of Financial Studies*, 2021) find **workplace injury rates fall 11–15%** after buyouts of publicly traded firms, with fewer OSHA violations, driven by monitoring and safety investment — not by outsourcing dangerous work.

---

**F4. The employment record is redeployment, not destruction — and in the most common deal type, PE is a strong net job creator**

*Moral basis:* Welfare, honesty about tradeoffs, consequences for workers as a class rather than anecdote.

*The argument:* The definitive employment study finds a nuanced truth the "job destroyer" narrative cannot survive. Buyouts of *publicly listed* firms — the minority of deals — do shrink employment (about 12% relative to controls over two years), typically at firms that were overstaffed relics of weak governance. But buyouts of *privately held* firms — the large majority of PE activity — **expand employment about 15% relative to controls**, because PE relieves the financing constraints that were choking growth. Meanwhile gross job creation *and* destruction both rise: PE accelerates the reallocation of workers from failing plants to growing ones, which is precisely how a healthy labor market raises wages over time. Judging PE by the layoffs alone is like judging medicine by the surgeries: the moral question is net outcomes for workers, and on net, across the full deal mix, employment effects are close to neutral-to-positive while productivity — the wage fund of the future — rises. And workers themselves gain durable human capital: employees of PE-acquired firms acquire transferable, technology-complementary skills that raise their long-run wages and employability *beyond* that firm.

*Evidence:* Davis, Haltiwanger, Handley, Lipsius, Lerner, Miranda (NBER w26371): employment **–12% after public-to-private deals but +15% after private-to-private deals** relative to controls. Boucly, Sraer, and Thesmar ("Growth LBOs," *Journal of Financial Economics*, 2011), on 839 French deals: post-LBO targets **grow faster than peers, increase capital expenditure, and add jobs**, especially when bought from individual owners in finance-dependent industries — PE as a credit-constraint relaxer. Agrawal and Tambe ("Private Equity and Workers' Career Paths," *Review of Financial Studies*, 2016): retained workers at LBO targets gain IT-complementary skills and experience **higher long-run employability and wages** than they would have absent the buyout.

**F5. The counterfactual: PE buys troubled, constrained, or ownerless firms — and its companies weather crises better, not worse**

*Moral basis:* Consequentialist honesty (compare against the real alternative, not an imagined one), rescue duty.

*The argument:* Critics attribute to PE ownership the distress that motivated the sale. But firms do not sell control at a premium when things are going well; PE disproportionately acquires companies that are declining, capital-starved, or strategically stuck — meaning the honest comparison is not "PE-owned firm vs. thriving firm" but "PE-owned firm vs. what was actually coming." The best evidence on this counterfactual comes from the harshest natural experiment available: the 2008 financial crisis. If PE's leverage made firms fragile, PE-backed firms should have died in disproportionate numbers. Instead they **invested more, grew assets faster, gained market share, and were no more likely to go bankrupt** than comparable firms — because PE sponsors injected equity and arranged financing when credit markets froze. Deep-pocketed, hands-on owners are an insurance policy for the firm's stakeholders precisely when it matters most. Named rescues make the point concrete: Continental Airlines was pulled out of bankruptcy in 1993 by a $450 million recapitalization led by Air Partners/TPG when no strategic buyer would touch it, then executed the famous "Worst to First" turnaround under Gordon Bethune, preserving tens of thousands of airline jobs. Blackstone held Hilton through the 2008 collapse, restructured the debt, invested through the trough, and exited in 2018 with the company larger and healthier than it bought it. KKR's 2007 Dollar General buyout — a struggling discounter — produced **2,000+ new stores and over 20,000 new jobs** with revenue up 56% by the time of its 2009 re-IPO.

*Evidence:* Bernstein, Lerner, and Mezzanotti ("Private Equity and Financial Fragility during the Crisis," *Review of Financial Studies*, 2019; NBER w23626): during 2008–09, PE-backed companies **decreased investment less than peers, received greater equity and debt inflows, grew assets and market share, and were not more likely to go bankrupt**, with effects strongest for financially constrained firms. Continental Airlines' 1993 Chapter 11 exit via the Air Partners (Bonderman/TPG)–Air Canada $450M investment (UPI archives, April 1993; Bethune, "Right Away and All at Once," HBR 1998). Hilton: $26B 2007 buyout, ~$14B realized profit by 2018 exit — value created by operational growth through a depression in travel, not by dismemberment (Bloomberg, May 2018). Dollar General figures per American Investment Council case study and contemporaneous Newsweek coverage.

---

**F6. The 1980s pension-reversion charge, examined honestly: legal recovery of employer surplus, accrued benefits paid in full, and a loophole Congress properly closed**

*Moral basis:* Property rights, rule of law, accuracy in moral accounting, institutional learning.

*The argument:* The most durable moral indictment of the LBO era — that buyout gains were "looted" from worker pensions — collapses under three facts. First, the law: under ERISA and the plan documents of defined-benefit plans, employers bore the funding risk (they had to make up shortfalls), and the residual surplus in an overfunded plan was, by the plans' own terms and Reagan-era 1983 guidelines, recoverable by the sponsor **only after every participant's accrued benefit was fully secured**, typically through purchased annuities. Terminating an overfunded plan did not take a dollar of any worker's *earned* pension; it recaptured the employer's own excess contributions and market gains. Whatever one thinks of the practice, "theft" is the wrong word for reclaiming a legally defined residual after paying all obligations. Second, the magnitudes: reversions were economy-wide corporate behavior (over $20 billion from ~2,000 plans, 1982–1990, mostly by ordinary corporations, not buyout funds), and the empirical literature could not substantiate the Shleifer–Summers "breach of trust" wealth-transfer hypothesis as the source of buyout premiums — Ippolito and James (*Journal of Finance*, 1992) found the data insufficient to support it, and Kaplan's work located the gains in operating improvements instead. Third, the system worked: Congress layered escalating excise taxes on reversions — 10% (1986), 15% (1988), and 20%/50% (Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1990) — which effectively ended the practice. A 35-year-old, legally closed episode, never shown empirically to be the engine of buyout returns, cannot carry the moral weight the anti-PE case loads onto it.

*Evidence:* GAO, "Pension Plans: Terminations, Asset Reversions, and Replacements Following Leveraged Buyouts" (HRD-91-21, 1990–91); reversion totals and the 1983 administration guidelines documented in the Indiana Law Journal's "Reversion of Surplus Pension Assets Upon Plan Termination" and American Academy of Actuaries' "ERISA at 50" (2024); excise-tax sequence in the Tax Reform Act of 1986, TAMRA 1988, and OBRA 1990. Shleifer and Summers, "Breach of Trust in Hostile Takeovers" (NBER w2342, 1988) is a theoretical conjecture; Ippolito and James (*Journal of Finance*, 47(1), 1992) found the wealth-transfer evidence wanting; Kaplan (*JFE*, 1989) traced buyout value to operating performance.

---

**F7. PE solves the succession problem: it is the buyer of last and best resort for founder- and family-owned firms that would otherwise wither or liquidate**

*Moral basis:* Consent and voluntary exchange, welfare of small-business owners and their employees, continuity of communities' economic institutions.

*The argument:* Most buyouts are not raids on public icons; they are negotiated purchases from founders and families who *want* to sell — to retire, to divide an estate, to fund the next chapter — and who have no heir willing or able to run the business. Without a control buyer, the realistic paths are a fire-sale to a competitor (who typically shutters overlapping operations), slow decline under a reluctant heir, or liquidation. PE provides what no other institution reliably offers these sellers: full liquidity at a competitive price, professional management, and growth capital. The starkest demonstration is Japan, where an aging society has produced millions of profitable firms with no successor — and where **succession-related transactions now account for more than 65% of buyout deals**, with PE openly credited with keeping viable family businesses alive. Every one of these transactions is consensual on all sides; condemning them means telling a 74-year-old founder she may not sell her life's work to the highest-committed bidder.

*Evidence:* Japan: ~2.5 million SMEs with managers over 70 lacking succession plans (CNBC, October 2025; EQT and AVCJ analyses); succession deals >65% of Japanese buyouts; Japanese PE deal value above ¥3 trillion (~$20B) annually for four consecutive years. Boucly–Sraer–Thesmar (*JFE*, 2011) show precisely these private-to-private, individual-seller deals are where post-buyout growth in jobs and investment is concentrated — the succession channel is the *job-creating* channel.

---

**F8. Creative destruction is a moral duty, not a vice: protecting incumbents and zombie firms taxes the young, the productive, and the future**

*Moral basis:* Justice between insiders and outsiders; consequences for unseen victims (workers of tomorrow, competitors, consumers); Schumpeterian welfare.

*The argument:* The alternative to reallocation is not a warm stasis in which everyone keeps their job; it is the slow entombment of capital and workers inside dying firms. The economics literature on "zombie" companies — kept alive by forbearance and cheap credit — shows they depress wages-to-productivity ratios, congest markets, and suppress the investment, employment, and productivity of *healthy* firms around them. Every job "saved" inside a zombie is paid for invisibly by workers never hired at the productive firm that couldn't expand. PE is one of the economy's principal institutions for refusing this bargain: it forces the redeployment of capital and labor from low-value to high-value uses, and the Davis et al. evidence shows exactly this signature — simultaneous surges in gross job destruction *and* creation with net productivity gains. A moral framework that counts only visible layoffs and never the invisible foregone jobs is not compassion; it is an accounting error that systematically favors incumbents over everyone else.

*Evidence:* Banerjee and Hofmann, "The Rise of Zombie Firms: Causes and Consequences" (*BIS Quarterly Review*, September 2018): zombie share of listed firms in 14 advanced economies rose **from ~4% in the late 1980s to ~15% by 2017**; zombie congestion measurably reduces healthy firms' investment, employment, and TFP growth. The canonical Japan evidence: Caballero, Hoshi, and Kashyap, "Zombie Lending and Depressed Restructuring in Japan" (*American Economic Review*, 2008). Davis et al. (NBER w26371) on buyout-driven reallocation with productivity gains.

---

**F9. Judged against the real alternatives — short-termist public markets, state ownership, or no buyer at all — PE is the most accountable steward available**

*Moral basis:* Comparative institutional ethics: institutions must be judged against feasible alternatives, not utopias.

*The argument:* Condemning PE implies some better owner was available. Who? Public markets impose quarterly-earnings myopia — in the famous Graham, Harvey, and Rajgopal survey ("The Economic Implications of Corporate Financial Reporting," *Journal of Accounting and Economics*, 2005), **78% of CFOs admitted they would sacrifice long-term value to smooth quarterly earnings** — and public markets are in any case shrinking as a venue: the number of U.S. listed companies roughly halved from its ~8,000-firm 1996 peak (Doidge, Karolyi, and Stulz, "The U.S. Listing Gap," *Journal of Financial Economics*, 2017), leaving thousands of mid-sized firms with no realistic public-market path. State ownership's record on productivity and honest capital allocation needs no rehearsal. Strategic acquirers buy to eliminate competitors and consolidate overhead — typically worse for target employees than a financial buyer who profits only if the standalone firm thrives. PE's five-to-seven-year horizon is *long* by public-market standards, its owners sit on the board and answer for results, and — unlike the LBO-era caricature — the Lerner, Sorensen, and Strömberg evidence shows it does not eat the future: patenting quality holds up and citations *rise* after buyouts. The honest comparison leaves PE not as a necessary evil but as the best available governance technology for a large class of firms.

*Evidence:* Graham–Harvey–Rajgopal (*JAE*, 2005). Doidge–Karolyi–Stulz (*JFE*, 2017) on the listing gap. Lerner, Sorensen, and Strömberg, "Private Equity and Long-Run Investment: The Case of Innovation" (*Journal of Finance*, 66(2), 2011): across 472 LBOs, **no decline in innovation; post-buyout patents are more highly cited** and more focused on core areas — direct falsification of the "sacrifices the long term" charge.

---

**F10. Consumers win: PE growth comes from new products and expansion, not price gouging**

*Moral basis:* Welfare of consumers (including low-income consumers), fairness of market conduct.

*The argument:* If PE's model were extraction, its portfolio companies would raise prices and cut quality. Large-sample scanner-data evidence shows the opposite mechanism: PE-backed consumer firms grow sales roughly 50% faster than matched controls, and essentially none of it comes from price increases (~1% on existing products) — it comes from launching new products and entering new geographies, i.e., competing harder. Combine this with Bernstein–Sheen's finding that PE-owned restaurant chains *cut* menu prices while getting cleaner and safer, and with Dollar General — a discount retailer serving low-income rural America that PE ownership expanded by thousands of stores — and the consumer-welfare ledger runs clearly positive. Serving customers better is not incidental to the PE model; under competition it is the only sustainable way to grow enterprise value.

*Evidence:* Fracassi, Previtero, and Sheen, "Barbarians at the Store? Private Equity, Products, and Consumers" (*Journal of Finance*, 77(3), 2022; NBER w27435): +50% relative sales growth, ~1% price effect, growth via new-product launch and geographic expansion, driven by eased financial constraints and managerial expertise. Bernstein–Sheen (*RFS*, 2016) on price cuts and sanitation. Dollar General expansion figures (AIC; Newsweek).

---

**F11. The consent argument: every link in the PE chain is a voluntary transaction between informed, sophisticated parties — and the most informed parties keep coming back**

*Moral basis:* Consent, autonomy, revealed preference, respect for fiduciary judgment.

*The argument:* No company is ever forced into a buyout: sellers are boards with fiduciary duties (public deals require shareholder votes, typically at 20–40% premiums), founders choosing their successor, or corporate parents divesting. The capital is committed by the most sophisticated, best-advised, most long-horizon investors on earth — pension boards, endowment investment offices, sovereign funds — who negotiate terms, audit performance across decades, and are legally bound to act in beneficiaries' interests. Their verdict is unambiguous: after forty years of data and every opportunity to exit, **more than 90% of institutional investors plan to maintain or increase PE allocations**, and CalPERS *raised* its target to nearly 18%. Paternalistic critics must explain why the fiduciaries with the strongest incentives and best information — the actual stewards of workers' retirement money — are all wrong, and why overriding the freely made, repeated choices of consenting institutions on behalf of their own beneficiaries would be morally superior. A practice sustained across decades by informed, voluntary, repeat participation on every side carries a presumption of legitimacy that its critics have to defeat — and the empirical record above shows they cannot.

*Evidence:* AIC pension studies (allocation and re-up intentions; CalPERS allocation and beneficiary count); Harris–Jenkinson–Kaplan (2014) net-of-fee outperformance as the material basis for that revealed preference; standard premia in public-to-private transactions documented in Kaplan–Strömberg (*JEP*, 2009).

# Part III. Synthesis

This section pairs the two cases point-by-point. It renders no moral verdicts. For each contested point it states where the sides agree on facts, what the actual crux of disagreement is (factual, causal, or moral), what the neutral factual record (Appendix) adds, and what second-order effects follow from accepting or rejecting the argument. Where an advocate's factual claim is contradicted or qualified by the record, that is flagged — flagging a factual error is not a moral verdict.

## S1. Pension "raiding" (A1 vs. F6; Appendix §§1–4, 6)

This is the complaint that motivated the investigation, so it gets the fullest treatment. The dispute decomposes into three separable questions that are often run together.

**S1a. The 1980s surplus reversions: what happened, and whose money was it?**

*Agreed facts:* Roughly $20 billion was reverted from ~2,000 terminated plans covering ~2.5 million participants in the 1980s. The practice was lawful under ERISA §4044(d) provided all accrued benefits were secured (typically via purchased annuities). Congress ended it with escalating excise taxes (10% in 1986, 15% in 1988, 20%/50% in 1990), and surplus-driven terminations have been essentially extinct since the early 1990s.

*Factual qualifications to both advocates:* The record qualifies A1's framing in one direction — most 1980s reversions were executed by incumbent managements (United Airlines, Exxon, A&P, Firestone), not raiders, and where reversions accompanied takeovers they financed on average only ~11% of the takeover premium (Pontiff, Shleifer & Weisbach 1990), so reversion was a takeover-adjacent tactic rather than the engine of LBO returns. It qualifies F6's framing in the other — the claim that termination "did not take a dollar of any worker's earned pension" holds only while the replacement annuity provider stays solvent. In the Cannon Mills and Pacific Lumber cases, the acquirers selected Executive Life annuities (in Pacific Lumber's case, over negative internal evaluations, because the cheaper annuity enlarged the reversion), and when Executive Life failed in 1991, retirees' earned-benefit checks were in fact cut. Workers also lost the funding cushion, expected future accruals, and surplus-funded ad-hoc COLAs even in reversions that went smoothly.

*The irreducible crux* is a property-theory question the facts cannot settle: was the surplus the employer's residual (the legal answer, strongest statement in *Hughes Aircraft v. Jacobson*, 525 U.S. 432 (1999) — the employer bears downside funding risk, so it is residual claimant on the upside) or workers' deferred wages (the union position — the cushion was bargained for in lieu of pay)? Accepting A1 requires the deferred-wages theory; accepting F6 requires the residual-claimant theory. Both are internally coherent; courts adopted the latter, which tells you what the law was, not which theory is right.

*Second-order effects.* If the worker-ownership view of surplus is accepted and enforced, employers lose the upside of overfunding, and the documented response is to stop overfunding: Ippolito (*J. Law & Econ.* 2001) found the reversion excise tax itself — the policy embodiment of the worker-protective view — discouraged deliberate overfunding and contributed to the thinner funding cushions of later decades, which in turn worsened the underfunded-plan failures of the 2000s. If the employer-residual view is accepted, sponsors can harvest cushions in good times, and workers bear annuity-provider risk (the Executive Life scenario) plus the loss of cushion at exactly the moment ownership changes. Either resolution redistributes risk rather than eliminating it; the design question underneath is who holds the buffer in a defined-benefit promise.

**S1b. Does the 1980s episode indict today's industry?**

The practice has been dead for ~35 years, killed by a tax that makes reversion economically pointless. The case against treats the episode as character evidence: the industry harvested pensions until forcibly stopped, then found the next mechanism — establishing a disposition, not just a closed incident. The case for treats it as institutional learning: a loophole existed, economy-wide actors (not primarily buyout funds) used it, the system closed it, and no modern mechanism exists for an acquirer to take accrued benefits or 401(k) assets (Appendix §6). Second-order context: accepting the character-evidence framing supports regulating PE prospectively, on propensity, before specific harms are demonstrated — which necessarily also burdens transactions that would have been benign. Rejecting it implies each new extraction mechanism is addressed only after harm occurs; the historical sequence both sides accept (reversions → excise tax; surprise billing → No Surprises Act; fee practices → vacated SEC rules) shows that reactive cycle typically runs years-to-decades per mechanism.

**S1c. The modern mechanism: bankruptcy, the PBGC, and withdrawal liability**

*Agreed facts:* The modern pension issue is the mirror image of the 1980s — shedding *underfunded* defined-benefit obligations through bankruptcy rather than seizing surpluses. The Sun Capital cases are real and as A1 describes them (Friendly's: the PBGC formally called the affiliate-repurchase structure an abuse; Marsh: ~$76M shortfall to PBGC; *Sun Capital v. New England Teamsters*: the 70/30 fund split defeated withdrawal liability in the First Circuit in 2019, though the 2013 holding that a PE fund can be a "trade or business" survives and now shapes deal diligence). It is also agreed that the largest pension defaults in history were non-PE (United Airlines 2005, Bethlehem/LTV Steel, Delphi, Sears under a hedge fund), that PBGC transfer is a bounded rather than total loss (80%+ of participants in trusteed plans receive full earned benefits; the affected minority averages ~24% cuts, concentrated among high earners and early retirees), and that 401(k) assets are legally unreachable by acquirers.

*The crux is causal and unresolved.* The best-evidenced channel is indirect: PE's leverage raises bankruptcy frequency roughly tenfold for large public-to-private deals (Ayash & Rastad 2021, contested at the margins, sample ends 2006), and bankruptcy is the doorway through which underfunded plans reach the PBGC. Whether PE ownership *causes* pension failure — versus PE selecting into already-distressed, old-economy firms whose plans were already underfunded — has not been cleanly separated by any study; the one direct paper (Zhong 2024) is a non-peer-reviewed working paper. Accepting A1's modern indictment requires crediting the causal reading; accepting F5's counterfactual defense requires crediting the selection reading; the honest state of the evidence supports neither with confidence.

*Second-order effects.* If the causal reading is accepted and policy responds by extending controlled-group pension liability to PE funds (reversing the *Sun Capital* outcome), funds face full exposure to portfolio pension debts — which would deter the deliberate liability-avoiding structures A1 condemns, but predictably also deter PE from buying companies with DB plans at all. What then happens to those companies depends on which side's counterfactual is right: under F7's view they lose their most likely buyer and decline toward liquidation (where the pension lands at PBGC anyway); under A1's view they escape an elevated bankruptcy risk. If the selection reading is accepted and nothing changes, the PBGC's premium structure — which does not price sponsor leverage — continues to let highly leveraged owners hold an underpriced put on pension obligations, a cost socialized across all other plan sponsors; both advocates' materials are consistent with this subsidy existing regardless of who is at fault for any particular failure.

*Scale note for calibrating the complaint:* the "raiding" concern today applies only to the shrinking set of targets that still have DB plans (~12% private-sector participation, most frozen); the typical worker's realistic exposures in a PE deal are job loss, wage effects, and severance (Toys "R" Us workers lost jobs and severance, not 401(k) balances), with PBGC transfer a tail risk. Meanwhile pensions sit on the other side of the ledger as PE's largest capital source (~35%). The friend's complaint is thus historically well-grounded for 1982–1990 (with the legality and incumbent-management qualifications above), and survives today in a transformed shape: not confiscation, but leverage-amplified bankruptcy risk plus legal structuring against pension liability.

## S2. Leverage: discipline or risk transfer? (A2, A4 vs. F2, F5)

*The factual puzzle:* Both sides' central empirical findings appear to be true simultaneously. Large public-to-private LBOs go bankrupt at ~10x the rate of matched controls (A4), *and* PE-backed firms weathered 2008–09 with more investment, more capital inflows, and no elevated bankruptcy rate (F5, Bernstein–Lerner–Mezzanotti). Possible reconciliations — different samples (large public-to-privates vs. all PE-backed firms), different eras, and sponsor behavior varying with whether supporting a company protects franchise value — are not settled in the literature. Neither advocate is entitled to generalize their finding across the whole industry.

*The crux:* whether debt that the target itself carries is a governance device consented to by everyone who priced it (lenders write the covenants; selling shareholders take the premium) or a mechanism for transferring downside to parties who never priced it (workers, trade creditors, pensioners, communities). The dividend-recap and portfolio-fee evidence (Payless, Simmons, Steward; the $20B in portfolio-company fees) is the strongest material for A2 because those extractions are unconditional on outcome; the crisis-support evidence is the strongest material for F5 because equity injections are the opposite of hit-and-run. The moral question underneath is who counts as a counterparty to the leverage decision.

*Second-order effects.* Restricting leverage or dividend recaps (e.g., minimum holding periods before distributions, as some proposed legislation would) shrinks buyout premia — which are paid substantially to selling shareholders, including pension funds — and reduces deal volume, with effects distributed per S1c's counterfactual dispute. Leaving the structure untouched leaves the elevated-bankruptcy externality where it lies, and channels reform pressure toward bankruptcy law itself (severance priority, retention-bonus limits), where several post-Toys-R-Us proposals have aimed. A middle observation both sides' materials support: lenders and sellers already price PE risk; the parties who cannot are exactly the ones bankruptcy law ranks last, so the incidence of any reform depends on bankruptcy priority rules at least as much as on PE-specific regulation.

## S3. Workers: breach of implicit contract or reallocation? (A5 vs. F4, F8)

*A rare case of shared evidence:* both sides rest on the *same study* (Davis et al., NBER w26371) and cite it accurately — the dispute is entirely about which margin morally counts. Public-to-private deals: employment −13%, earnings −1.7%, pre-buyout wage premium largely erased. Private-to-private deals (the majority of activity): employment +15%. Productivity rises ~8% on average.

*The crux* is the status of the implicit contract. A5 (via Shleifer–Summers) holds that the erased wage premium was earned — deferred consideration for loyalty and tenure — so extracting it is a transfer, not a gain, and the beneficiaries of the productivity increase are not the people who paid for it. F8 holds that protecting incumbent arrangements taxes invisible outsiders: the zombie-firm literature (BIS 2018; Caballero–Hoshi–Kashyap 2008) documents that capital and labor locked into low-productivity firms suppress the wages and employment of everyone else. These positions do not contradict each other factually; they weigh different people.

*Second-order effects.* A regime that enforces implicit employment contracts (making acquirers assume pre-existing wage premia and staffing norms) protects incumbent workers and blunts the reallocation mechanism, with the zombie-congestion costs falling on workers never hired — costs that are real in the literature but invisible in any case study. A regime that treats implicit contracts as unenforceable teaches workers that deferred, trust-based compensation will not be honored across ownership changes — pushing labor bargains toward spot-market cash compensation, which is more portable but strips the long-tenure bargain some workers and firms prefer. The employment-effect heterogeneity also means any across-the-board policy misfires somewhere: rules calibrated to public-to-private extraction burden the private-to-private deals where PE is measurably a job creator, and vice versa.

## S4. Vulnerable sectors: why the evidence splits (A3, A10 vs. F3, F10)

*The striking pattern* — visible only when the two cases are set side by side — is that the operational evidence divides cleanly by market structure. Where the customer can observe quality and exit (restaurants, consumer products, discount retail), PE ownership measurably improves sanitation, safety, and prices (Bernstein–Sheen; Cohn et al.; Fracassi et al.). Where the payer is government and the customer is captive or cannot judge quality (nursing homes, hospitals, ERs, housing for credit-constrained tenants), PE ownership is associated with higher mortality, more hospital-acquired conditions, surprise billing, and eviction-as-collections (Gupta et al.; Kannan et al.; Cooper et al.; FTC v. Invitation Homes; Atlanta Fed). Both bodies of work are methodologically serious; neither refutes the other. The most economical description consistent with both: the model amplifies whatever incentive gradient a market presents — toward quality where quality is rewarded, toward extraction where extraction is invisible or inescapable.

*The crux:* A3 reads the healthcare results as revealing the model's essence, with the consumer-sector results as the special case where competition happens to discipline it. F3/F10 read the consumer results as the essence, with healthcare as a special case of broken payment design that distorts *any* profit-maximizing owner (noting for-profit non-PE operators show some similar patterns, though the cited studies find PE effects over and above ownership type). The evidence as gathered here does not adjudicate which is the essence and which the exception.

*Second-order effects.* Accepting the sector-essence reading supports categorical exclusions (bans or strict review of PE in healthcare, as several states have enacted transaction-review laws). Consequences run both directions: distressed hospitals and homes lose a class of willing acquirers (whether alternatives exist varies by market — Hahnemann's fate under a non-PE investor suggests the buyer's structure mattered as much as its category), while the documented mortality and billing harms are foreclosed. Accepting the payment-design reading supports payer-side fixes that discipline all owners — the No Surprises Act is the existence proof both sides' materials acknowledge: it ended the surprise-billing strategy without banning anyone, though only after the harm had operated for years and against industry-funded opposition (A9). The choice of reading thus largely determines whether reform targets who may own care assets or how care is paid for.

## S5. Who actually gets the returns? (A6, A7 vs. F1, F11)

*Where the numbers actually stand (Appendix §5):* The apparent head-on collision — "13.5% net, best asset class" vs. "matches an index fund" — is mostly an era and benchmark dispute. Pre-2006 vintages beat the S&P 500 by ~3%/yr net (Harris–Jenkinson–Kaplan, largely uncontested). Post-2006 vintages roughly match public indices in the same authors' data — the fight is over benchmark choice (S&P 500 vs. leveraged small-cap value), risk adjustment, and whether "highest absolute return in the portfolio" (true for many pensions) is the relevant claim when the alternative was cheap beta. Ennis's public-plan work finds alternatives-heavy allocations produced *negative* alpha net of the full cost stack since 2008. F1's headline figures come from an industry trade association (AIC); A6's come from the industry's most prominent academic critic; the Appendix rates the post-2006 question genuinely contested, with the skeptical position gaining academic ground.

*The consent crux:* F11's revealed-preference argument (sophisticated fiduciaries re-up for decades) collides with A7's evidence that the SEC found fee violations or material control weaknesses at over half of examined managers, and that CalPERS could not compute its own carry paid. The question a reader must resolve: how much undisclosed-fee evidence does it take before "informed consent" stops describing the LP relationship? Note the two sides' pension arguments are not symmetric in kind: F1 says pensions *gain* from PE (fund-level returns); A1/A7 say pensions are *harmed* by PE (portfolio-company-level dumping, fund-level skimming). Both can be true at once, even for the same pension fund.

*Second-order effects.* If the no-alpha reading is accepted and pensions rotate out of PE, the direct effect on beneficiaries is roughly index-like returns with more liquidity and lower fees (by that reading's own math), the fee transfer to GPs shrinks, and the buyout sector's capital base — and every effect in S1–S4, good and bad — scales down with it. Underfunded pensions relying on 7%+ actuarial assumptions would face pressure to either cut assumed returns (raising required contributions from governments) or find yield elsewhere. If the outperformance reading is accepted, divestment campaigns (e.g., those aimed at pension boards after Toys "R" Us) impose real costs on the very workers they invoke. The fee-transparency question is severable from the returns question: mandatory disclosure (the vacated SEC rules) would inform the consent F11 relies on, at compliance costs the industry argued — successfully, in the Fifth Circuit — exceeded the SEC's authority to impose.

## S6. Carried interest and the political economy (A8, A9 vs. F11)

*Agreed facts:* Carry is taxed at capital-gains rates; economists across the spectrum overwhelmingly classify it as labor income; the treatment has survived repeated closure attempts under both parties amid heavy industry lobbying; estimated revenue at stake is tens of billions per decade — material as a fairness question, small relative to federal revenue.

*The crux* is less the tax analysis (thinly defended even by the industry on the merits) than what the loophole's persistence signifies: A8/A9 read it as purchased law — wealth recycled into preserving the rule that generates the wealth, which corrodes the "fair market verdict" framing of every other pro-PE argument. The strongest available counter-framing is procedural: lobbying is lawful petitioning, and the rule's survival reflects legislative choice. A reader's verdict here tends to color how they read every consent- and legitimacy-based argument on both sides.

*Second-order effects.* Closing the loophole would likely be partially priced back into fund economics (higher fees or restructured compensation, borne partly by LPs — i.e., pensions) and partially reduce GP net income; estimates on the split differ. Leaving it open sustains the reciprocity objection and keeps the issue available as a recurring political mobilizer against the industry — a cost the industry itself bears in regulatory risk. The opacity arguments (A9) interact with everything above: most of the empirical disputes in S1–S5 persist *because* private structures produce less data; transparency mandates are therefore partly a meta-policy about making the other questions answerable.

## S7. The comparative-institutions frame (A9 vs. F9)

*Agreed facts:* U.S. public listings have roughly halved since 1996; public-market managers self-report short-termism; PE's 5–7 year horizon is long by quarterly standards; innovation quality holds up post-buyout (Lerner et al. 2011).

*The crux:* F9 argues institutions must be judged against feasible alternatives, and for a large class of firms the feasible alternatives (shrinking public markets, strategic buyers who consolidate, no buyer) are worse for stakeholders. A9 answers that PE's advantage over those alternatives consists substantially of *escaped accountability* — the comparison flatters PE because the alternatives are load-bearing parts of the disclosure and liability regime PE arbitrages around. These framings assign the same facts opposite valences: "less constrained" is efficiency in one and impunity in the other.

*Second-order effects.* Accepting F9's comparative frame directs reform toward making public markets more attractive (listing-cost reduction, litigation reform) rather than restricting PE — on the theory that a better alternative disciplines PE more effectively than rules do. Accepting A9's frame directs reform toward extending public-company-style obligations (disclosure, controlled-group liability) into private ownership — which narrows the regulatory-arbitrage gap at the cost of the flexibility that, on F9's account, is the reason a buyer exists for these firms at all. The two programs are not mutually exclusive, but they compete for the same political capital, and each side's materials predict the other's program will be captured or evaded.

## Closing note: what would actually resolve the open questions

The synthesis above leaves the genuinely moral cruxes (surplus ownership, implicit contracts, who counts as a counterparty, what consent requires) to the reader, as intended. But several load-bearing *factual* disputes are resolvable in principle, and a reader deciding how much weight to place on each argument may want to track them: (1) a causal study separating PE's effect on pension-plan failure from selection into distressed sponsors (the current evidence is one working paper); (2) replication and extension of the bankruptcy-rate finding past 2006, where the crisis-resilience evidence points the other way; (3) benchmark-robust post-2006 return estimates from parties without a stake (the current antagonists are a trade association and a dedicated critic); (4) whether healthcare-quality deterioration is PE-specific or attaches to any leveraged for-profit owner under the same payment design. Each of these would move at least one synthesis section off "unresolved."

# Appendix. Private Equity and Pension Funds: A Factual and Legal Brief

*Prepared as a neutral fact-finding review. Confidence flags: items marked **[verified]** were confirmed against sources during this research; **[widely reported, not independently verified here]** means the claim is standard in the literature but the primary figure was not re-confirmed; **[contested]** means credible sources disagree.*

## 1. The 1980s practice: terminating overfunded plans and reverting the surplus

**What the practice was.** Under ERISA as enacted (1974), an employer could voluntarily terminate a defined-benefit (DB) plan, satisfy all *accrued* benefits (typically by purchasing annuities), and — if the plan document so provided — take back ("revert") any residual surplus under ERISA §4044(d). The 1980s bull market plus high interest rates (which shrink the present value of liabilities) left many plans heavily overfunded — the typical plan in 1986 held roughly $125 of assets per $100 of liabilities. In 1983 the Reagan administration issued interagency guidelines that clarified and effectively eased the reversion route (Congressional Record, 1995; Indiana Law Journal note, "Reversion of Surplus Pension Assets Upon Plan Termination").

**Scale.** Reversions grew from ~$404 million (1982) to ~$6.7 billion (1985); over the 1980s, more than **$20 billion was recaptured from roughly 2,000 plans covering ~2.5 million workers and retirees** (figures cited in the Congressional Record, Nov. 8, 1995, and contemporaneous GAO work). A 1988 Christian Science Monitor account put the running total at $16+ billion since 1981.

**Connection to LBOs and takeovers — real but partial.** This is the most important nuance. The academic benchmark is Pontiff, Shleifer & Weisbach, "Reversions of Excess Pension Assets after Takeovers," *RAND Journal of Economics* (1990): in a sample of 413 takeovers, reversions occurred after **15.1% of hostile takeovers vs. 8.4% of friendly ones**, and where they occurred they financed on average ~11% of the takeover premium — meaningful, but "too small to be the sole, or even dominant, source of takeover gains." GAO reviewed 558 DB plans at 121 companies acquired in LBOs (GAO/HRD-91-21, 1991); related GAO testimony (T-HRD-89-20) found ~40% of reviewed LBO companies terminated plans holding ~$1.8 billion in assets, reverting ~$581 million. So: reversion was a takeover-adjacent tactic, but **most 1980s reversions were done by incumbent managements, not raiders**, and most LBOs did not involve reversions.

**Headline cases.** United Airlines (UAL) announced plans to recapture ~$962 million in 1985 (Washington Post, June 30, 1985, "'Recapturing' of Pension Assets Stirs Debate"). Other frequently named reverters: A&P, Firestone, Reynolds, Harper & Row (Chris Welsch, "Pension reversions," Substack survey of the era). Raider-associated cases: Charles Hurwitz/MAXXAM's 1985 Pacific Lumber takeover (terminated the pension, used surplus toward deal debt, replaced annuities through Executive Life, which later failed — a long-running grievance); Carl Icahn's TWA (plans frozen 1993; Icahn personally on the hook for funding, per Washington Post, Oct. 25, 1992). Exxon's 1986 termination is commonly cited as the largest single reversion (~$1.6 billion) **[widely reported, not independently verified here]**.

**Was it legal? Yes.** Reverting surplus after paying all accrued benefits was lawful under ERISA §4044(d), provided plan-document and annuitization requirements were met. Workers did not lose *accrued, vested* benefits in a standard reversion — what they lost was the funding cushion, expected future accruals, and frequently ad-hoc COLA increases that surpluses had funded. The major worker harms materialized when replacement annuity providers failed (Executive Life, 1991) or replacement plans were inferior.

**Whose money was the surplus? Legally the employer's; morally contested.** The legal theory: in a DB plan the employer bears downside funding risk, so the employer is residual claimant on the upside. Unions and worker advocates argued the surplus was deferred wages — a cushion bargained for in lieu of pay. Courts largely sided with employers. The Supreme Court's *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson*, 525 U.S. 432 (1999), unanimously held that an employer's use of surplus (there, in an ongoing plan partly funded by *employee* contributions) did not violate ERISA's anti-inurement rule — the strongest statement that participants have no entitlement to surplus beyond their defined benefits. **[Verified; the normative dispute remains genuinely contested in the literature.]**

## 2. The legislative response, and whether the practice ended

- **Tax Reform Act of 1986**: 10% excise tax on reversions.
- **Technical and Miscellaneous Revenue Act of 1988**: raised to 15%.
- **Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990**: raised to **20% if the employer establishes a qualified replacement plan or shares surplus with participants, 50% otherwise** — the current regime, codified at IRC §4980 (verified against the U.S. Code). Reversions are also ordinary corporate income, so the combined effective take on a no-replacement-plan reversion exceeds 70–80%.

**Did it end? Effectively, yes.** Academic work (Ippolito, "Reversion Taxes, Contingent Benefits, and the Decline in Pension Funding," *Journal of Law & Economics*, 2001) finds the 1986 tax alone cut termination-for-reversion by ~36%, and the 1990 rate made reversion economically pointless in nearly all cases. Ippolito's second-order finding is worth flagging: the reversion tax also **discouraged deliberate overfunding**, contributing to thinner funding cushions in later decades — an unintended consequence sometimes cited on both sides of the policy debate. Surplus-driven raids of the 1980s type have been essentially extinct since the early 1990s; modern surplus extraction happens through narrow lawful channels (qualified replacement plans, §420 transfers to retiree health).

## 3. Modern mechanisms linking PE ownership and pensions (named cases)

The modern issue is the mirror image of the 1980s: not seizing surpluses, but **shedding *underfunded* DB obligations**, typically via bankruptcy.

**(a) Portfolio-company bankruptcy → distress termination → PBGC.** When a plan is terminated in distress, PBGC trustees it and pays benefits up to statutory caps (2026 single-employer max: ~$93,477/yr, ~$7,790/mo, for a 65-year-old straight-life annuity; much lower for early retirees, and supplemental/shutdown benefits are largely unguaranteed). The haircut question: per the CRS PBGC Primer (report 95-118, updated June 2026), **more than 80% of participants in PBGC-trusteed single-employer plans receive their full earned benefit; among those cut, the average reduction is ~24%**, concentrated in a handful of large plans (airline pilots with high salaries and early retirement ages were the classic losers). So PBGC transfer is a real but bounded loss for most workers — plus loss of all *future* accruals.

Named PE cases:
- **Friendly's (Sun Capital, 2011)** [verified]: Chapter 11 four years after Sun's buyout; PBGC formally accused the structure of being engineered "to leave the pension plan abandoned and seriously underfunded" while a Sun affiliate re-purchased the company via credit bid — a "sale to yourself" that shed the ~6,000-participant plan. A settlement let the sale proceed; PBGC assumed the plan; Sun retained ownership (Boston Globe, Dec. 13, 2011; PLANSPONSOR; CEPR, "Sun Capital's 'Gift' to Taxpayers").
- **Marsh Supermarkets (Sun Capital, 2017)** [verified]: PBGC absorbed a ~$76 million shortfall for ~4,000 participants after the grocer's collapse (Indianapolis Business Journal). Widely covered reporting (NYT, 2017) emphasized that Sun had extracted value via real-estate sales during ownership; that characterization is contested by the firm.
- **Harry & David (Wasserstein & Co., 2011)** [verified]: bankruptcy judge approved terminating the DB plan over PBGC's objection that the company's survival did not require it; the sponsor's PE owner conditioned exit financing on shedding the plan (Bloomberg Markets, Nov. 2011; PBGC filings, Case No. 11-10884, Bankr. D. Del.).
- **Contrast cases showing the pattern is not PE-specific**: the largest DB dumps in history were non-PE (United Airlines 2005 — largest default ever at the time; Bethlehem/LTV Steel; Delphi 2009; Sears 2019, ~$1.4B shortfall, under hedge fund ESL). And PBGC has won when it could prove evasion: **PBGC v. Renco Group (RG Steel)** settled in 2016 with Renco — a family holding company, not a PE fund — taking the plans *back*, only the second plan restoration in PBGC history (PBGC press release PR16-01).

**(b) Multiemployer withdrawal liability — the Sun Capital litigation** [verified]. *Sun Capital Partners III & IV v. New England Teamsters & Trucking Industry Pension Fund* (D. Mass./1st Cir., 2012–2019): at stake was whether PE funds are jointly liable, as members of a "controlled group" of "trades or businesses" under ERISA/MPPAA, for a portfolio company's pension debts — here $4.5M of withdrawal liability from bankrupt Scott Brass Inc. (Sun Fund IV 70%, Fund III 30% — a split many read as deliberately staying under the 80% control threshold). The First Circuit's **2013** ruling held a PE fund *can* be a "trade or business" (the "investment plus" test) — a landmark against the passive-investor defense. The district court (2016) found the two funds a "partnership-in-fact" and liable. The **First Circuit reversed in November 2019**: no partnership-in-fact, funds not liable (No. 16-1376). Net result: Sun won on the facts, but the 2013 trade-or-business holding stands, so PE funds holding ≥80% of a company with pension obligations still face controlled-group risk — now a standard item of deal diligence (McDermott; Proskauer; Paul, Weiss client alerts).

**(c) Freezes.** Freezing DB accruals post-acquisition is lawful, common, and not PE-specific — it has been the dominant corporate-America move since the mid-2000s (IBM, Verizon, GE, none PE-owned). No systematic evidence isolates a PE-specific freeze rate. **[Evidence thin.]**

## 4. Causation: does PE ownership *cause* pension failure?

The honest answer: **the causal evidence is limited, and the strongest documented channel is bankruptcy risk, not pension-targeting per se.**

- **Bankruptcy channel (strongest evidence).** Ayash & Rastad, "Leveraged Buyouts and Financial Distress" (*Finance Research Letters*, 2021; SSRN 3423290): of 467 large public-to-private LBOs 1980–2006, ~**20% went bankrupt within 10 years vs. ~2% of matched controls** — a roughly tenfold difference. Since distress termination requires bankruptcy, higher leverage mechanically raises the probability a DB plan lands at PBGC. This paper's methods were disputed by Davis, Haltiwanger, Kaplan et al. ("Private Equity, Jobs, and Productivity: Reply to Ayash and Rastad," HBS working paper) in a separate exchange about employment; the bankruptcy-rate finding itself is less contested but the sample ends in 2006. **[Contested at the margins.]**
- **Direct pension evidence (thin, recent).** Zhong, "How Do Private Equity Buyouts Affect Employee Pension Plans?" (SSRN 4818003, 2024) finds buyouts are followed by deteriorating plan funding, with worse effects in PBGC-insured plans — consistent with moral hazard (underpriced PBGC insurance as an implicit put). This is a **working paper, not peer-reviewed**; treat as suggestive.
- **The critical literature.** Appelbaum & Batt, *Private Equity at Work* (2014), documents the case pattern (Friendly's, Harry & David, etc.) and the incentive structure. Critics respond that these are selected cases, not base rates.
- **Confounders.** (i) The secular collapse of DB coverage — private-sector DB participation fell from **38% (1980) to ~20% (2008) to ~12% (2019)** (SSA *Social Security Bulletin* 69(3); BLS) — happened overwhelmingly at non-PE firms. (ii) PE disproportionately buys already-distressed and old-economy businesses (selection); turnaround shops like Sun Capital specialize in companies whose pensions were often underfunded before acquisition. (iii) The biggest PBGC claims in history are non-PE (airlines, steel). No study cleanly separates "PE caused the failure" from "PE bought companies likely to fail" for pensions specifically. **[Genuinely unresolved.]**

What *is* well supported: PE's high-leverage model raises bankruptcy frequency, bankruptcy is the doorway through which underfunded plans reach PBGC, and PBGC's flat-ish premium structure means the downside is partly socialized across other plan sponsors.

## 5. The other side of the ledger: pensions as PE investors

- **Share of capital.** Pension funds are the **largest LP segment in private equity — roughly 35% of all capital in the asset class** per Preqin's 2020 Global PE Report, with estimates of 35–40% of global commitments persisting through 2025 (Preqin). U.S. public plans (CalPERS, CalSTRS, Texas TRS, Washington SIB, etc.) are the anchor investors; the practical irony noted even by critics is that the LPs funding buyouts include the retirement systems of public workers (In These Times, on Toys "R" Us and pension-fund divestment pressure).
- **Returns — the mainstream finding.** Harris, Jenkinson & Kaplan, "Private Equity Performance: What Do We Know?" (*Journal of Finance*, 2014): U.S. buyout funds **outperformed the S&P 500 by ~3%/yr net of fees for pre-2006 vintages**. Kaplan & Brown (2019) reaffirm historical outperformance.
- **Returns — the critical finding.** Phalippou, "An Inconvenient Fact: Private Equity Returns & The Billionaire Factory" (*Journal of Investing*, 2020): **since roughly the 2006 vintage, net-of-fee PE returns (~1.5–1.6x money multiples, ~11%/yr) approximately match public equity indices**, while GPs collected an estimated $230 billion in carry. Notably, Harris-Jenkinson-Kaplan themselves find post-2006 convergence — the sharpest disputes are over benchmark choice (S&P 500 vs. leveraged small/mid-cap value), risk adjustment, and starting-vintage selection (industry rebuttals from Apollo, Blackstone, KKR, Carlyle are appended to Phalippou's paper; American Investment Council, "Correcting the Record").
- **Fund-level drag.** Ennis (*Journal of Investing*, 2024-25 work; richardmennis.com): 54 large public plans generated **negative alpha of ~1.2%/yr since 2008**, attributable to alternatives' cost stack (he estimates ~5%/yr all-in cost for PE); each 1-point allocation shift to alts associated with ~7 bps lower excess return. Counterpoint: several large plans' own reports (and CEM Benchmarking data cited by the industry) show PE as their highest-returning asset class in absolute terms. Related integrity concern: Brown, Gredil & Kaplan, "Do Private Equity Funds Manipulate Reported Returns?" (*JFE*, 2019) find some underperforming funds inflate interim NAVs, though investors mostly see through it. **[Contested; benchmark-dependent. Fair summary: pensions have earned solid absolute returns from PE; whether they beat an appropriate public benchmark net of fees since ~2006 is disputed, with the skeptical position gaining academic ground.]**

## 6. What happens to ordinary workers' retirement money in a typical PE acquisition today

- **401(k)s: essentially safe from the acquirer.** Defined-contribution assets sit in an ERISA trust (§403), legally separate from corporate assets, unreachable by the employer, its creditors, or a buyer, and survive bankruptcy intact. In Toys "R" Us — the era's canonical bad-outcome PE bankruptcy — workers' 401(k) balances were not lost; the losses were **jobs and promised severance**, partially addressed by a discretionary $20M fund from KKR and Bain (vs. ~$75M workers claimed) (CNBC/CNN/CBS, Nov. 2018). Residual DC risks are mundane: suspended matches, plan-merger frictions, and concentration in employer stock (an Enron-type risk, not PE-specific).
- **DB pensions: a tail risk, conditional on two gates.** First, the target must still *have* a DB plan — increasingly rare (~12% private-sector participation, most already frozen). Second, loss requires **bankruptcy plus distress termination**; short of that, accrued vested benefits cannot legally be cut, and even at PBGC, 80%+ of participants receive full earned benefits (average ~24% cut for the minority affected, worst for high earners and early retirees; unguaranteed shutdown/supplemental benefits are the exception — see RG Steel). Freezes (stopping future accruals) are the *common* outcome and are lawful, but they mirror economy-wide practice.
- **The 1980s scenario — a buyer seizing pension money — is dead.** The 50% excise tax plus corporate income tax forecloses it; there is no modern mechanism for an acquirer to take workers' accrued benefits or 401(k) assets.
- **Bottom line.** For a worker at a typical PE-acquired company today, *pension confiscation* is not a realistic outcome; *loss of future DB accruals* (freeze) is common where a plan exists; *PBGC transfer with possible haircut* is a tail risk correlated with the elevated bankruptcy rates documented above; and the best-evidenced worker risks in PE deals run through **employment, wages, and severance**, not retirement accounts.
